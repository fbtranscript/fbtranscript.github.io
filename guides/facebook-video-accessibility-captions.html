Facebook Video Accessibility: Captions and Transcripts

Why captions and transcripts matter for accessibility, and how to use them well.

Captions and transcripts are not just a convenience; they are an accessibility feature that opens videos to people who are deaf or hard of hearing, and to anyone watching without sound. They also help people who are learning the language or who process text better than audio. This guide explains the accessibility basics every viewer and creator should know.

Who benefits from captions

Captions help people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people in noisy or quiet places, and people learning a new language. They also help search engines and screen readers understand a video. A video without captions excludes a large audience, often without the creator realising it.

What good captions look like

Good captions are accurate, well timed, and easy to read. They identify speakers when it matters, describe important sounds such as applause or music, and use a readable font size and contrast. They should not cover important visuals, and they should keep up with the speech without rushing.

Transcripts as an alternative

A transcript is a text version of the whole video, including descriptions of visual content when it matters. It is useful for people who use screen readers, and for anyone who wants to search or skim the content. Offer a transcript alongside the video whenever you can.

Creator responsibilities

If you post videos, add captions before you publish, review them for errors, and provide a transcript for longer videos. Test your video with the sound off to see whether it still makes sense. Accessibility is not an extra; it is part of making a video properly.

Putting it into practice

Start with a single, short example and work through the steps above from beginning to end. Do not try to do everything at once, and do not use an important or irreplaceable video for your first attempt. Once the first try works, repeat the process with a second example, and you will find the steps become familiar quickly. Keep your files organised as you go, give them clear names, and check the finished result on the device where you plan to use it, whether that is your phone, your computer, or a tablet.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is rushing: pasting the wrong link, picking the lowest quality, or downloading from the first site that appears in a search without checking whether it is trustworthy. The second is ignoring storage and file names, which leaves you with a folder full of files called download-1 and download-2 that you cannot tell apart. The third is forgetting the creator: always credit the person who made the original, keep personal copies personal, and ask before you reuse someone else's work in public.
