How to Get a Transcript of a Facebook Live Video

Transcripts for Facebook Live: replays, captions, and post-live options.

Facebook Live videos are watched in real time, but many people catch up later on the replay. A transcript helps those viewers, and it helps the creator reuse the content. This guide explains how captions and transcripts work for live videos, both during the stream and afterwards.

Captions during a live

Facebook can generate live captions for many broadcasts, showing the spoken words as they happen. The accuracy depends on the audio quality and the speaker's clarity, so live captions are often rougher than captions on an uploaded video. Viewers can turn them on with the CC button during the stream.

Transcripts from the replay

After a live ends, the replay is saved as a normal video, and the same caption and transcript options apply. If the live had captions, the replay usually keeps them. You can then download or copy the captions, or transcribe the replay audio with a speech-to-text tool.

Transcribe a live yourself

If no captions exist, record the audio during the live, or use the replay, and run it through a transcription tool. Long lives produce long transcripts, so consider transcribing in sections and labelling each part. A summary of the key points is often more useful than a full transcript of a two-hour stream.

Plan for accessibility in advance

Before you go live, test your microphone, reduce background noise, and speak clearly. Tell viewers that captions are available, and consider having a moderator post key points in the comments. After the live, add a corrected transcript to the replay description so late viewers can catch up.

Putting it into practice

Start with a single, short example and work through the steps above from beginning to end. Do not try to do everything at once, and do not use an important or irreplaceable video for your first attempt. Once the first try works, repeat the process with a second example, and you will find the steps become familiar quickly. Keep your files organised as you go, give them clear names, and check the finished result on the device where you plan to use it, whether that is your phone, your computer, or a tablet.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is rushing: pasting the wrong link, picking the lowest quality, or downloading from the first site that appears in a search without checking whether it is trustworthy. The second is ignoring storage and file names, which leaves you with a folder full of files called download-1 and download-2 that you cannot tell apart. The third is forgetting the creator: always credit the person who made the original, keep personal copies personal, and ask before you reuse someone else's work in public.
